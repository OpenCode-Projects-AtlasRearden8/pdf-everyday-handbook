What a PDF Really Is

Here is the single idea that untangles most PDF confusion: a PDF is a print job, printed on invisible paper.

When you saved that Word document "as a PDF," your computer called the printer driver and said: here is what this would look like printed — now print it to a file instead of to paper. That file is the PDF. Once written, it is frozen. It does not re-flow, re-paginate, or re-word-wrap like a document in Word or Google Docs. It is a description of ink positions on fixed pages.

This is why:

  • A PDF looks the same on every machine. Positions are fixed. That is the whole point of the format.
  • Editing a PDF is hard, the way editing a photograph of a document is hard. The file has ink, not intentions.
  • A two-page document can become a four-page PDF, because the "print job" broke the pages where it thought it should, not where you would have.

What is actually inside a PDF

Think of a PDF as a folder open on a desk:

  • Page descriptions — the "ink": letters drawn as shapes, lines, and images, each with an exact position.
  • Fonts — the letter shapes. Sometimes the PDF embeds the fonts it needs (it carries the exact shapes with it, so it looks right everywhere). Sometimes it merely mentions a font by name, and your computer substitutes its closest guess. That is why one PDF renders crisply and another renders "almost right."
  • Images — pictures embedded as photographs, each with its own resolution. This is almost always where the file size comes from.
  • Metadata — a little inventory card on the front of the folder: title, author, creation date, which program made it. Not always truthful! Chapter 08 shows how to read it and why never to trust it blindly.
  • Structural bits — a how-to-find-things map (the "cross-reference table") saying where each object lives in the file. If this map is subtly damaged, some viewers shrug and open it anyway while others refuse. A PDF can be technically readable and technically a bit broken, at the same time.

The two families of PDF

Every future chapter in this book depends on which family your PDF belongs to:

  1. Born-digital PDFs — created by a word processor, export button, or PDF-maker. The letters are real text. You can select a sentence with your mouse, the file size is small, and your AI can read it directly. The text can be extracted.
  2. Scanned (image-only) PDFs — a photograph of paper, one picture per page, wrapped in a PDF jacket. You cannot select the text because there is no text — there is only flat pixels. Your AI can't read it either, until OCR (chapter 07) converts the picture back into text.

The two-second test: drag your cursor over a paragraph. If a chunk of text highlights, it is born-digital. If nothing highlights, it is a scan. Do this test first every time; it instantly tells you which set of tools applies. (Chapter 08 builds this test into a full inspection routine.)

The three things people get wrong most

  1. Assuming the filename means anything. A file called contract_signed.pdf is whatever its content is — possibly still blank. Extensions are labels on the outside of the folder, not the contents.
  2. Believing a PDF is "done" because it looks done. Pages break across boundaries. Fields stay unfilled. The print preview is page 12 of the trust ladder — use it, every time you are about to print or submit.
  3. Confusing size with importance. A 25 MB PDF is usually just several large photos in a trench coat. A 300 KB PDF can be a legally binding, perfectly complete contract. Size tells you about the images, not the seriousness.

Why every chapter after this gets easier

Once you hold the "print job on paper" idea, the rest of the book is just applying it:

  • Forms (chapter 02): some print jobs come with interactive placeholders — wells waiting for ink. Filling is either using the wells or printing the page and adding your own.
  • Compressing (chapter 03): the photos in the folder are heavy; the text is nearly weightless. Compression mostly means treating the photos.
  • Signing (chapter 04): a printed signature is photograph-style honesty; a digital signature is cryptographically sealed tamper-evidence. Different jobs, different tools.
  • Merging and splitting (chapter 05): you are rearranging the pages of a folder — the cover page stays, the fax cover sheet nobody wanted goes in the shredder.
  • Extracting text and OCR (chapters 06 and 07): born-digital PDFs give you text back free; scans need OCR to earn it.
  • Inspecting (chapter 08): you are reading the folder's inventory card, and deciding whether contents match claims.

One small idea. One whole, much calmer relationship with your paperwork. Now go fill in a form.

Fill In Any Form (Without Paying for Software)

You should never need a paid subscription to fill in a form. Repeat that out loud if you have ever been cornered into a monthly plan by a flashing "UPGRADE TO COMPLETE" banner. The free, mostly-local paths below cover nearly every form you will meet.

First, know which kind of form you have

  • Interactive form — it has real answer fields (wells waiting for ink). Click a spot and a box highlights. Firefox, Chrome, Adobe Reader, and Preview on Mac can type into these and save the result, all free, all local.
  • Flat form — a PDF that looks like a form but has no fields at all; it is really a photograph or drawing of a form. No software can "fill" it, because there is nothing there to fill. You simply stamp your answers on top (next section).

Not sure which you have? Open the PDF and click around on an answer line. A highlight or a caret means interactive. Nothing at all means flat. Twenty seconds, and you now know the right tool.

Path A: interactive form — use a free local tool

Pick whichever is already installed:

  1. Firefox — open the form, fill it in, then File, Save. Done. (Firefox is the sneakiest good choice; it even saves typed answers if you need to come back later.)
  2. Chrome or Edge — fill in, then use the PDF viewer's own Save.
  3. Adobe Acrobat Reader — the free (Reader) edition fills and saves forms. Only ever install Reader, never the trial of paid Acrobat. If an installer pushes a trial, cancel and reinstall the plain Reader.
  4. Mac Preview — the Markup tools do text boxes, checkmarks, and a signature from trackpad or a photo of your ink signature, which sounds like a hack but works beautifully on flat forms too.

Remember: if the tool warns the form "cannot be saved with your data," see the flatten trick below.

Path B: flat form — stamp your answers on top

You are adding ink to a photograph. Two good ways:

  1. Write on the scan (annotate). In Preview (Mac) or Firefox/Edge annotations, add a text box for each answer, sized roughly to the printed line. Tedious for big forms; perfect for a few blanks.
  2. Print, write by hand, scan back. Old but unbeatable for handwriting-style answers, sketches, and initials-in-margin forms. Scanning steps live in the companion printer-and-scanner-handbook. Coming back digital: the result is a scan, so it is in the scanned-PDF family — treat it accordingly (chapters 07 and 08).

For asking-someone-else-to-type jobs, LibreOffice Draw (free, desktop) can open many flat PDFs and place text boxes directly on the page; save the result back as PDF with a fixed layout.

The flatten trick — make your filled answers permanent

Some forms arrive as interactive, but the recipient's software ignores your typed answers when it re-opens the file later. The fix: once the form is filled, re-save it so the ink is painted onto the page, not stored in fields.

  1. Fill the form in Firefox or Reader.
  2. Print it to PDF ("Print, choose Save as PDF" — no paper involved).
  3. The printed-to-PDF copy shows every answer as plain ink. That is the copy you send.

This is the closest thing PDFs have to "permanent marker." It is also how you guarantee what-you-see is what-they-see, on any viewer.

When the fields just will not behave

Field boxes refuse to appear? Nine times out of ten the file was flattened by whoever made it, or the fields live on pages after your viewer stopped loading. Try in order:

  1. Open in a different viewer (Firefox if you tried Reader, Reader if you tried Firefox).
  2. Check whether that page is actually a scan hiding inside a mostly-interactive PDF. Scan pages are flat forever.
  3. Give up gracefully and use Path B. Your time is worth more than the elegant solution.

A privacy word, since we promised one

The big-browser-tool space is full of sites that say "upload your form, fill it online, download it free!" Read that with chapter 01 in mind: you are uploading a document full of your personal data to somebody else's server. Sometimes that trade is worth it (a public, non-personal document, a tool you trust). Often it is not. Everything in this chapter can be done on your own machine. Do that whenever the form contains your name, income, address, license numbers, or anything medical. Local-first is not a slogan; it is a boundary.

Shrink a PDF to Meet an Upload Limit

"File exceeds maximum size" is the enroll-your-kid-blocker of our age. Here is the calm, mostly-local way to make any PDF smaller — plus the wisdom to know when smaller is worse and it is time to call the human on the other end of the portal.

Why your PDF is big (usually the pictures)

Chapter 01's rule: text is nearly weightless; images are heavy. A 20 MB PDF of typed pages contains almost no text at all by weight — it contains photographs of pages (scans) while each photograph is stored at full quality, or it contains a handful of embedded photos snapped on a phone camera.

So shrinking a PDF is mostly about treating the pictures, in three honest ways:

  • Downsample — store the picture at a lower dots-per-inch than you need.
  • Recompress — store the same picture with stronger compression.
  • Remove — delete pages (e.g., scans of blank backs) or unused extras.

The two-minute path (works on any computer, no install)

  1. Open the PDF and choose Print.
  2. Choose the built-in Save as PDF (Mac) or Microsoft Print to PDF (Windows) printer.
  3. If your print dialog has a "quality" or "images" setting, drop it to medium / 150 dpi.
  4. Print to a new PDF. Keep the original! Check the new one still matches (chapter 08's checklist).

This re-prints the whole document at the print engine's pace, which quietly does some downscaling. It is not the strongest compression in the world, but it is free, offline, and often drops a bloated scan-PDF by half or more. On Linux, the "Print to PDF" printer plus CUPS options does the same job.

The strong, still-free, still-local path: Ghostscript

Ghostscript is a free, open-source PDF post-processor with real compression muscle. One command:

gs -sDEVICE=pdfwrite -dCompatibilityLevel=1.4 -dPDFSETTINGS=/ebook -dNOPAUSE -dQUIET -dBATCH -sOutputFile=small.pdf input.pdf

What the important parts mean, in plain English:

  • -dPDFSETTINGS=/ebook = "target 150 dpi images, fine for screens" (the sweet spot for upload portals).
  • Use /screen for the most aggressive squeeze (72 dpi — fine for reference copies, risky for signatures; test readability after).
  • Use /prepress if you need print-shop quality and are fighting the limit anyway (rare).
  • On Windows the command is the same with gswin64c instead of gs (part of the free Ghostscript installer).
  • On macOS and Linux, install Ghostscript from your package manager; the command runs in Terminal.

Then compare file sizes, and — important — open small.pdf and check it is still readable and complete. Compression is not magic; it is a controlled loss. You control how much loss is acceptable; the checklist in chapter 08 tells you what to verify.

Trim the fat before you compress

  • Delete blank or duplicate pages. Scans with a blank back-of-page, or "same doc saved twice," shrink instantly when you use a page rearranger (chapter 05 covers free page tools).
  • Split and send the biggest, then the rest. If a portal accepts one file per field, you may not need to compress at all — the size limit is usually per file.
  • Not the file's fault? Then it is the photo's fault. Retape, reshoot or rescan the original at a sanely lower resolution (phone scanning apps often ask for quality level; medium is plenty for text) and you win before compressing.

Reading the winner's numbers like a pro

For most portals, here are the rough size targets, translated into real life:

  • Under 1 MB — almost always fine; this is a screen-readable scan of typed pages.
  • 1 to 5 MB — fine for most portals; page images around 150 to 200 dpi.
  • Over 10 MB — usually means full-resolution photos are inside. There is nearly always room to shrink without anyone noticing, unless the PDF contains real photographs needed as evidence (property photos, ID documents, medical scans).

When to stop squeezing and email a human instead

Sometimes the right move is a different door, not more compression:

  • The PDF is irreplaceable artwork or a signed medical image where quality loss matters.
  • You have squeezed with /ebook and /screen and it is still over the cap. The cap may be the wrong axis (the portal may also have a page limit or a count-of-files limit you have missed).
  • The portal says "must be under 2 MB" and your file is 2.1 MB after honest compression — email or call; portals get adjusted by humans all the time.

The most important line in this chapter: always keep the original file. Compressed copies work down from originals. Delete the original and you have deleted your ability to fix, re-check, or re-submit anything.

Sign It: Print-Sign-Scan vs. Careful Digital Signing

A signature is a claim: I agreed to this. Both of the approaches in this chapter can carry that claim honestly — but they carry it in different ways, and mixing them up causes real problems. Let's get the difference straight so you always pick on purpose.

Print-sign-scan: the ink-and-paper classic

You print the PDF, sign with a real pen, scan (or photograph) the pages back into one PDF, and send it. Courts, employers, and landlords accept this daily. What a scrawled scan actually proves, though, is weaker than it feels: it proves somebody's ink was on the page at some point. It does not prove who, or when, or what happened to the file afterwards.

That weakness can be just fine — a signed lease between you and your landlord needs to be truthful and accepted, not cryptographically armored.

Steps that make print-sign-scan smooth (details for the hardware itself live in the companion printer-and-scanner-handbook):

  1. Print only the pages that expect your signature if the rest is 40 pages of contract boilerplate; know exactly where your initials and date go before you hit print. Signature-required pages usually tell you, but circle each signature and date line with pencil before printing, so you do not mail back an unsigned page 14.
  2. Sign every clearly-marked line, and date it on the day you sign.
  3. Scan straight-on, even page-by-page, black text mode or color as the original requires — do not crop so tightly that a margin note disappears; the context around your scrawl is part of the proof.
  4. Merge back into a single PDF in the original page order (chapter 05 covers this in detail).
  5. Re-read it once, asking "would I be embarrassed if a stranger saw this?" before sending.

Careful digital signing: ink, upgraded with math

When people say "digital signature" they may mean three genuinely different things. Know which one is happening:

  1. A typed or drawn signature image placed on the page. This is the drag-and-draw-your-name stage in e-signature services, Adobe Fill & Sign, Mac Preview's Sign tool, or an image created from a photo of your cursive. It is the visual ritual, and it is no more technically tamper-proof than the scanned version. It is fine for most everyday agreements, especially when the counterparty uses the same service checkbox ("this is my signature") as part of their acceptance flow.
  2. A cryptographic digital signature with a certificate. This is the real deal: your signing identity plus a certificate, so the file itself carries tamper-evidence. If so much as a comma changes after signing, the file announces it as invalid. Ordinary people meet this through government-issued electronic ID programs (several countries give every citizen one for free), employer-issued signing identities, or a certificate from a verification service. Tools that can apply such a signature include Adobe Acrobat (paid), LibreOffice (free), and various government ID apps.
  3. An e-signature service with legal-wrap agreements. The signature itself is a drawn stack, but the service keeps an audit trail: who clicked, from which account, at what timestamp, with which IP address. Courts in many places treat this trail as the actual signature. You are trading some privacy and a little cash for a strong legal shield. Choose it when the deal is big enough to matter.

Choosing on purpose: which one for which job?

  • Everyday, low-stakes, inside-your-community agreements: print-sign-scan or a drawn signature image — whichever you have at hand. Truthfully said and mutually understood beats cryptographically perfect every time.
  • Employment, housing, financial agreements: drawn signature image inside a reputable e-signature service both sides already use, or a real certificate-signed PDF if you have the cert.
  • Anything where the other side could be tempted to alter the file later — contracts with a stranger, final records tied to money or property, medical consents — ask for a service that produces an audit trail, or a certificate-signed file, and keep the email thread that quotes the crucial paragraph back to you.
  • Legal or high-stakes paperwork where you suspect fraud risk: certificate-signed. Full stop.

The gotchas this corner of the book exists for

  • A "signed" PDF is only as protected as the tool that handled it after signing. If someone re-saves a signed file through a converter or viewer that rewrites it, the drawn or scanned signature can survive visually while the protection underneath quietly breaks or disappears. Verify the final version before you rely on it or send it.
  • E-signature services sometimes legally do not cover the type of document you have. Read the service's own one-pager about what is excluded (wills, some real property transfers, some medical consents in the US) before you rely on it.
  • Never re-sign somebody else's already-signed document. If a counterparty must also add their signature, start from a copy of the unsigned file that both sides agreed on. With paper signing, everyone watches the same page; with audit-trail services, the service tracks the signing order for you.
  • If a portal demands a specific kind of signature (some government and bidding portals insist on certificate-signed PDFs), ink will be rejected no matter how beautiful. Budget the time to get the real signing identity once, rather than fighting rejections forever.

One privacy word

A scanned or drawn signature is a copy of you that you then share with every recipient and their servers. If you wouldn't want your handwritten signature public, use a service-side drawn signature rather than a scan of your actual cursive, or sign with a stylus rather than a phone photo of your hand. Real ink, real you, real risk.

Merging and Splitting for Packet Uploads

Most portals do not want a PDF. They want one tidy PDF that contains your whole packet in the right order — cover sheet first, form second, proof third, extra evidence last — under the size cap, with no fax-cover-sheet ghosts from 2011 lurking on page 7. This chapter is how you build such a packet calmly, and the worksheet in chapter 09 gives it to you as a print-and-pencil checklist.

The tools, all local and free

  • PDF Arranger (Linux/Windows; free) — drag, drop, rotate, delete, reorder pages; save as a new PDF. The friendliest tool for this job, full stop.
  • Mac Preview — the sidebar shows page thumbnails; drag to reorder, drag pages between two open PDFs, File, Export as PDF to save the merged result.
  • Command line, when it becomes a weekly chore — qpdf (free, everywhere) merges and splits in one line:
qpdf --empty --pages doc1.pdf 1-3 doc2.pdf -- packet.pdf

That line says: start with nothing, take pages 1 to 3 of doc1, then all of doc2, save as packet.pdf. Swap tool for pdftk (also free) in pdftk A=doc1.pdf B=doc2.pdf cat A B output packet.pdf — same idea, slightly different grammar. One of these will be installed already or is a one-minute install away.

Merging: the six-step calm ritual

  1. Gather the parts. Every document gets its own properly named file in one folder before merging: 01-application-form.pdf, 02-insurance-card.pdf, 03-payslips-sept.pdf. (Numbers in the names are a gift to your future self; they make the final order obvious to any human and any tool that lists files alphabetically.)
  2. Check the page shape. Everything landscape or everything portrait? Mixed orientation is fine, but viewer-side flip-flopping isn't. Rotate the odd ones first, inside the page tool rather than re-scanning (the printer-and-scanner-handbook explains why re-scanning costs quality).
  3. De-duplicate. If your insurance card is in two documents, decide which one is the good copy and delete the other from the working set.
  4. Put no cover sheet inside another document. The cover sheet becomes page 1 of the packet PDF, not page 1 of an inner file, or the portal's packet-preview will look like a stack of brochures.
  5. Merge in order using a page tool (see PDF Arranger / Preview / qpdf above) into one file: packet-final.pdf.
  6. Do the final flip-through. Open the packet, page through at reading pace — not skimming, reading — and confirm order and orientation. Only then send it.

Splitting: one-thing-per-upload, the clean way

Portals that accept one file per field need each document split back out. Both directions of the same knowledge:

  • Split by page range. In PDF Arranger, export selected pages as a new file (right-click, Export). In qpdf:
qpdf --split-pages big.pdf out-%d.pdf

(Makes several files, each with the maximum page count the portal usually allows; to hand-split a range, qpdf --empty --pages big.pdf 4-9 -- form-part.pdf.)

  • Split by logical document, not by page count. If big.pdf has 40 pages but the real documents inside it are "form (pages 1-8), insurance (9-10), paystubs (11-20)," extract each range into its own file. A page-count-max split does not fool a human clerk who needs the insurance card to be its own upload.
  • After splitting, always page through what you are about to upload. It takes 30 seconds and catches the flipped or missing page that got you told "incomplete submission" two times already this year.

The size cap collides with merging: the escape hatches

Merging usually increases total size — photos pile up. When your packet busts the upload limit:

  1. Compress before merging, not after, so you compress once, knowingly, at the page level. Chapter 03's Ghostscript one-liner is your friend.
  2. Or, if the portal allows only some merged size, split into packet-one (the required docs, compressed /ebook) and packet-two (extras), and upload packets separately per field.
  3. Never re-scan to fix a size problem — re-scan always makes things bigger. Compress or re-export, and consult chapter 03.

Page-order and packet sanity habits worth building

  • Alphabetical-but-numbered filenames numbered pages 01, 02, 03 stay in the order you intend.
  • Names that a stranger (or the clerk, or you in six months) can visually classify without opening the file: 03-payslips-oct2026.pdf beats scan0147.pdf. Labels outside the packet make the inside of the packet findable.
  • Keep a copy of the sent packet, with the day's date on it (2026-10-29-coop-insurance-packet.pdf), even when the portal returns a receipt. In disputes, this copy is your receipt.

Take the worksheet in chapter 09 with you on your next packet job. It is designed to be printed once and used, pencil-style, table-side; the first time you build a big packet with it, you will wonder how you ever shipped one unpaginated.

Pull Text Out of a PDF (and Feed It to Local AI)

There comes a day when you want a machine to do something useful with your paperwork: "summarize this lease," "find every deadline in these fourteen pages," "compare the two quotes side by side." All of it starts with one step: turn the PDF into text the AI can actually read. This chapter is that step — done safely, locally, and for free.

Remember the two families (this is the chapter where it matters)

Chapter 01 taught the test; here is why it decides everything:

  • Born-digital PDF — the letters are real text. Extracting is a free lunch, done in seconds.
  • Scanned PDF — a photo of paper wrapped in a PDF jacket. There is no text in the file to extract; the machine sees only shapes. This family needs OCR first (chapter 07).

Nothing else in this chapter works until you know which family you are in. Drag-select a paragraph. Highlights? Born-digital, continue. Nothing? Scanned — jump to chapter 07 and come back.

The free-and-local extraction menu

  1. Copy-paste. Honestly: select, copy, paste into your text editor. Fine for a paragraph or two. Watch out for weird characters when you paste ("fi" and "fl" sometimes arrive fused, quotation marks arrive in strange guises, line breaks land mid-sentence) — for a paragraph, clean-up is a ten-second relief.
  2. pdftotext (from the free Poppler tools). The old, reliable, offline standard, built for this exact job. Installed on most Linux; one homebrew or installer away on Mac and Windows.
pdftotext -layout lease.pdf lease.txt

The plain-English part: -layout means "keep the two-column and table arrangement the way a human laid it out," which usually makes AI summaries better because tables stop being a jumble of identical rows.

  1. That's it, honestly. For text extraction, pdftotext is the graceful unsung hero. Commercial extractors mostly re-wrap the same capability, and web-based "free PDF to text" services just move the same job onto a stranger's server — see the privacy note.

Feeding extracted text to a local AI, safely

Once you have clean .txt (or markdown copies of the text), local-first AI is genuinely local: your document never leaves your machine. Tools like Ollama, LM Studio, or llamafile run language models entirely on your own computer.

  1. Have the text ready in a file you can point to (lease.txt from pdftotext, or a copy-paste from one chapter's worth of text).
  2. Load a decent model in your local AI app. For reading leases, paystubs, contracts, insurance PDFs, casual small docs — a mid-sized model you can run comfortably on your machine is fine. Ask the app or hub guide for its current recommendation; the model menu changes by the season.
  3. Paste or attach the text, then ask human-shaped questions: "What dates in this document matter?", "What am I promising?", "What is the riskiest sentence here you'd want a lawyer to check?" — plain language, in, out.
  4. Extract only what you need. Whole-30-page-PDFs as context can waste the model's attention (some models can't "see" all of a long paste at once). Give it the pages that actually matter: chapter 05's splitting knowledge (qpdf --empty --pages big.pdf 9-14 -- just-the-relevant-part.pdf, then pdftotext that) pays off again here.

With a local model, the classic privacy fear is solved structurally: nothing was uploaded anywhere. Do keep normal computer hygiene — anyone with access to this computer can still read what you asked it.

The small print of extracted text

  • Formatting isn't just decoration — tables collapse or misalign, footnotes run underneath, page numbers go stray. Skim the .txt before feeding it to the AI; five seconds of tidying often upgrades the answers.
  • Some born-digital PDFs still extract badly — ornate layout programs can save each word as individually positioned shapes, which extraction tools then get in the wrong order.
  • **A scanned PDF will extract to an empty or near-empty .txt file.** That's the family-mix-up check, not a bug: no text was in the file. OCR first, then extract.
  • Private but not secret: a local model processes within your machine, but keep your own habits tidy — the extracted .txt files linger in your folder. Delete or archive them like any other copy of your documents.

One chapter, one promise

Do this right, and AI reads your paperwork like a friend would — carefully, locally, and without anyone's cloud getting a copy. That is the whole idea this handbook's AI chapters stand on.

OCR in Plain English

OCR — "optical character recognition" — is the trick that turns a photograph of text back into text. If that explanation lands, you already know what it does; the rest of this chapter teaches you the two things everyone actually needs: when you need it, and how to run it free, offline.

When a PDF needs OCR, and when it does not

  • Born-digital PDF (you can drag-select a sentence; the file was made by an export button) — no OCR. Ever. The text is already text. Running OCR on it would be a suspiciously expensive way to degrade something that was free and perfect.
  • Scanned PDF (nothing draggable; each page is one flat picture) — this is OCR's home. The pixels go in, words come out.

Chapter 01's drag test, again, in a sentence: you cannot work with what you cannot see, and OCR is how the machine gets to see.

What OCR actually does (plain English, thirty seconds)

The computer looks at each page-image and asks, over and over: is this shape an e? an f? a comma? Then it stitches the letters into words, the words into lines, and lays them down as real text positioned to match the picture. That's it. It is pattern-matching done at industrial scale and speed.

What follows from that simple description:

  • It can be wrong. Blurry scans, weird fonts, handwriting, coffee stains — every imperfection of the originals is a chance to guess a wrong letter. Check the output against the picture when the stakes are high.
  • It works best on clean, straight, typed text. Low-contrast, skew-jewed, smudgy photos reduce accuracy. Rescanning gently beats OCR-ing desperately; the printer-and-scanner-handbook has the scan settings.

The free, local, mature stack: Tesseract and OCRmyPDF

  1. Tesseract is the free, open-source OCR engine: the actual shape-reader. Install it once via your package manager (the hub guide's setup section covers your operating system; Mac users typically install via Homebrew).
  2. OCRmyPDF is the friendly wrapper built around Tesseract, specialized for our main job — it adds the text layer into the same PDF (an invisible layer of selectable text sitting under each page photo), so the PDF stays a PDF and becomes both things at once: photo on top, text underneath.
ocrmypdf --language eng scanned.pdf searchable.pdf

Plain-English translation: "read scanned.pdf assuming English words, and write searchable.pdf, same pages, but now with searched-from text." Add --language eng+fra if the document mixes in a second language — a hint that genuinely improves accuracy.

Then the magic moment: open searchable.pdf, and drag-select a paragraph. It highlights now. You have converted a member of the scanned family into a hybrid — and once the text is in there, chapter 06's pdftotext works on this PDF, and your local AI can read it.

Practical prices and trade-offs

  • Time. Tesseract processes a typed page in a second or two on ordinary hardware. A 300-page lease takes some minutes — let it run, check your email instead, come back.
  • Accuracy. Fine scans of typed text: usually excellent, 99-ish percent. Phone-photos at an angle: noticeably worse; straighten (OCRmyPDF has --deskew for a light auto-straighten) and re-shoot if the whole document matters.
  • Language and handwriting: English typed is the sweet spot; other languages work when you tell OCRmyPDF which ones to expect. Neat handwriting produces tolerable results with patience; truly terrible pens beat OCR outright, and a human reading it is faster than a machine guessing.
  • File size: the text layer adds a whisper of data; the page photos stay the same weight. This is not be confused with compression (chapter 03) — you can do both in sequence on the same file.

A homesick note on trust

OCR output is a best guess with proof, not a certification. If the extracted text says "$1,850 per month" and your eyes look at the page photo and see "$1,350 per month," believe the page photo, not the OCR. This same principle extends to feeding local AI documents: scan-then-OCR text feeding an AI should come with a "verify the numbers" disclaimer — worth remembering even for summaries and deadlines.

And that is all of OCR you need for ordinary life: test the PDF first; OCR only when it is actually a photo-of-text; use free local tools, not someone else's upload server; check the output with your own eyes.

Is This PDF What It Claims to Be?

Every so often, a PDF arrives wearing a costume. It says final_contract_signed.pdf and contains an unsigned draft. It claims to be a bank statement and is a random document renamed. It looks fine in the email preview and falls apart in the portal. This chapter is the short, calm inspection routine — a pre-flight check before you trust a PDF someone else sent, and before you send a PDF of your own.

The 60-second inspection (do this every time before you trust or send)

  1. Read the file, not the filename. What is this really? Open it. Filenames are labels, not guarantees — chapter 01's first rule. statement.pdf might be a photo of a prompt in a web browser; receipts_scan_2026.pdf might contain one receipt and nine pages of blank backs.
  2. The drag test. Select some text. Highlights mean born-digital; empty cursor means scanned family. Now you know whether the text is real and extractable — remember, this decides what you can do with the document later.
  3. Count the pages, glance at every page. Is the expected page order there? Missing last page? Page 2 and page 3 swapped? Page rotation sideways for two pages in the middle? Every mis-matched page is a re-do request waiting to happen. Page through at reading speed, not skim speed; the portal's thumbnail strip counts page images but not page content.
  4. Check every page's readability. Text legible? Photos dim but usable? Scan slanted? Signatures visible and on the correct lines? A compressed-but-unreadable page costs you another email round-trip, same as a missing page.
  5. Look at the metadata (the "inventory card" of chapter 01). Right-click the file: Properties (Windows), Get Info (Mac). Viewer menus show PDF metadata on documents too (in Firefox: File, Properties or in Adobe Reader: Document Properties). What usually matters: which program made it, when, is the title or author something unexpected, and did a different page get appended by a different program at a different time. This is a hint, not truth — the fields are user-editable before upload, so they can be misleading, but a suspicious mismatch ("Author: DMV Forms System" on a personal letter) is worth a raised eyebrow.
  6. If the document carries a claimed digital signature, verify it, don't admire it. A file can show a realistic-looking signature image while having no valid cryptographic signature underneath at all. Where it matters, check what the viewer reports about the signature's validity, not just the ink on the page (chapter 04 explains the difference).

Sending: the same 60-second check, backwards

Before you send a PDF of your own, use the same checklist as the receiver's first impression:

  1. Rename honestly. 2026-10-29-coop-insurance-packet.pdf — dates and content, not final_v7_REAL.pdf. The reader finds it in six months, thanks you.
  2. Re-check page order, rotation, completeness in one steady pass.
  3. Strip anything that was not meant to travel. Click File, Properties; if the metadata lists tracked-page notes or a stranger's sign-off, decide on purpose whether it should travel. Metadata edits are legal; some privacy-strict portals blanch at freighting.
  4. Confirm form fields really saved. Chapter 02's flatten trick, if needed.
  5. Check size against the portal's cap (chapter 03) — and confirm visually that the compressed copy still matches the original page-for-page.

Signs of a PDF worth being suspicious about

In your life, the facts will probably never be interesting enough to use this section. That is fine; it is your seatbelt for the day they are:

  • A document where page order files DO NOT simply continue: e.g., page 5 of "Appendix" comes after page 9 of "Exhibit B."
  • File contents inconsistent with the email that carries them: statement attachment where the stated date is wrong.
  • A document where the file was a photograph of a computer screen — the resolution and horizon lines tell you. Good question: why was it a photograph?
  • Multiple CreationDate fields, or metadata claiming two entirely different producer programs with no story for the gap.
  • The signature's visible ink and the tamper-evidence stamp disagree about what was signed.

Any of these deserve a plain-language question to the sender: "I want to double-check this PDF's history before I rely on it." Most honest senders simply re-send. A good-faith question in email is the cheapest fraud filter in the world — and it costs zero megabytes.

Why this routine matters more than any single tool

Chapter upon chapter, every operation in this handbook — filling, compressing, signing, merging, extracting — can go quietly wrong, and every failure mode produces the same verdict: a PDF that looks okay and isn't. The inspection routine above is your handrail over all of it. Two minutes of looking, before you send and before you trust, beats two weeks of un-winding anything else.

Part 2: Pre-merge checks (5 minutes)

  • Every part passes the 60-second inspection (chapter 08): real pages, right page counts, no blank backs, no swapped pages.
  • Every part has been renamed honestly (date and content, starting with a number for order).
  • Every scanned part that a human or AI will need to search later has OCR applied if it is image-only (chapter 07). Born-digital parts need nothing here.
  • Signatures verified on every page that expects them: signed, dated, visible, on the right lines (chapter 04). Sign before merging into the packet.
  • Forms flattened if they were interactive and the fields-won't-save gotcha applies (chapter 02).
  • Decide: does the portal size cap (chapter 03) need pre-merger compression? If any single part is over the cap, compress that part first, not the merged packet.

Part 3: Merge (the calm ritual, in one place)

  1. Rotate the odd-page-orientation parts first so the packet reads upright throughout (PDF Arranger, Preview, or re-export).
  2. Merge in the numbered order from Part 1 into one file: packet-final.pdf (qpdf, PDF Arranger, or Preview — chapter 05).
  3. Name the merge with a date and a purpose: 2026-11-15-home-loan-packet.pdf.

Part 4: Post-merge inspection (2 minutes, non-negotiable)

  • Page order matches the manifest exactly (Part 1, left to right).
  • Page-through at reading speed; orientation, dark scans, and sideways text all caught here.
  • File size is under the portal cap with room to spare.
  • Signatures are still visible after compression (if you compressed; a squash can blur faint ink).
  • The copy you are about to send is your dated copy — one kept on your side even if the portal keeps its own.

Part 5: After sending (30 seconds)

  • Portal receipt / confirmation number saved to your notes or files.
  • Dated copy of the sent packet retained (year-month-day plus purpose, sorted where you'll find it again).
  • Working files (unmerged parts, compressed intermediates) archived or binned — your folder, spouse's eyebrows, and future self all benefit.

One-page peace of mind. Fill it in during the coffee, merge it that same night.

Glossary

Every term this handbook leans on, translated into human. Terms link back to what matters, but the table is designed to be read on its own — 2 minutes, and jargon stops being a wall.

TermPlain-English meaning
PDFPortable Document Format — a file that describes where every page of "ink" sits on fixed pages, so the same pages show up the same way everywhere. A print job saved as a file.
Born-digital PDFA PDF whose words are real text (made by an export button or PDF-maker). You can select its text, the file is light, and your AI can read it straight away.
Scanned (image-only) PDFA photograph of pages, wrapped together in a PDF jacket. No actual text inside — just pixels — until OCR is applied.
PageOne fixed "sheet" inside a PDF. A file has a page count; nothing re-flows it.
FieldA pre-drawn placeholder box in a form PDF where an answer goes. Some PDFs have real fields; others only look like they do.
FlattenTo bake a filled form's typed answers (or field boxes) permanently into the page as plain ink, so they show up the same everywhere.
AnnotationA drawn or typed mark placed on top of a PDF page (a text box, highlight, checkmark). Annotations usually stay a separate layer.
MetadataA PDF's small inventory card: title, author, creation date, which program made it. Readable, useful as a hint, and easy for a sender to edit before they send.
CompressionMaking a file smaller by storing its pictures more economically. Text is nearly weightless; compression mostly means treating images.
Lossy compressionCompression that throws detail away on purpose (standard for photos and page scans). Fine while pages stay readable; see chapter 03's checklists.
GhostscriptThe free, open-source command-line tool used for real PDF compression in this handbook (gs, or gswin64c on Windows).
DPI (dots per inch)Image density. Lower on scans = smaller file and coarser look. 150 dpi is usually "fine for screens"; higher is for print shops.
Signature (ink-style)Your physical or drawn signature as an image on the page. Proves agreement to humans; adds no tamper-protection at all.
CertificateA file proving "this digital identity was verified by somebody" — the credential you need for real, math-protected digital signatures.
Digital signature (cryptographic)A signature protecting the file itself with a certificate: any change after signing breaks the seal and is announced as invalid.
Audit trailAn e-sign service's diary of who signed, which account and timestamp, IP address. Courts often treat the trail, not the ink smudge, as the signature.
Tamper-evidenceThe property that says "if any part of this PDF was changed after signing, somebody can detect it."
MergeCombining two or more PDFs into one, in a chosen page order — your packet (chapter 05).
SplitCutting a PDF into pieces — by page count or, better, by logical document.
Cross-reference tableThe internal index inside a PDF that tells a viewer where each object lives in the file. If this index is damaged, some viewers open the file anyway while others refuse — a PDF can be technically readable and technically broken at once.
pdftotextThe free, offline tool for pulling the text back out of born-digital PDFs (Poppler suite).
OCR (optical character recognition)The trick that turns a photograph of text back into actual text, by asking the computer "is this shape an e?" a few million times per page.
TesseractThe free, open-source OCR engine this handbook recommends.
OCRmyPDFThe friendly wrapper around Tesseract that adds a searchable text layer into a scanned PDF without changing its pages or size much.
Searchable PDFA scanned PDF that has quietly gained an invisible text layer: photo on top, select- and search-able text underneath.
Local AIA language model running entirely on your own computer (Ollama, LM Studio, llamafile). Your documents stay home.
ExtractionChapter 06's whole job: getting the words back out of a PDF so tools and AI can read them.
PortalThe website that says "please upload a PDF under 2 MB," then stores whatever actually arrives. Now you know how to feed it right.
